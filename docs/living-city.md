# Gold Dynasty — Living City and Contracts

Design proposal for the game

## Everyday play

Players can walk into shops, cafés, venues, workshops, and other accessible interiors and use them as part of daily life. Order food to restore health or drink coffee for a short energy boost and faster stamina recovery. Keep survival light so players can spend their time on the activities they choose. Doors, vehicles, equipment, and everyday objects should support meaningful interactions where the world allows them.

## Contracts and livelihoods

Let players earn money, make connections, switch careers, work multiple jobs, own businesses, hire other characters, and award contracts themselves. A one-off job can become recurring work, a partnership, or a bidding opportunity. Players can build a satisfying life in any of these paths.

| Role or industry | Example work and progression |
| --- | --- |
| Agriculture and food | Harvest and sell produce, supply a café, bid for hotel accounts, or build a food brand and hire growers. |
| Fishing and marine trades | Fish for leisure or income, sell a catch to a restaurant, fulfill a seafood supply deal, captain a charter, or run a boatyard. |
| Diving and salvage | Inspect a yacht, recover lost cargo, survey a wreck, or manage a dive crew. |
| Treasure hunting | Accept a museum commission, fund a private expedition, or negotiate with a family over a relic they claim. |
| Entertainment | Perform at a club, earn a residency, compose for a production, negotiate sponsorships or label deals, or hire and book performers. |
| Content creation and news | Film local stories, cover events, take commissions, build an audience, report on an unfolding incident, or run a production company. |
| Security | Take personal protection or event shifts, form a firm, staff jobs, and bid on work for festivals, estates, marinas, or ports. |
| Repair and transport | Restore classic cars, rent out vehicles or equipment, run deliveries, or expand into a garage or dispatch business. |
| Underground economy | Fictional syndicate contacts can offer waterfront or citywide courier jobs. Reputation, debts, promises, and relationships determine who offers work and how the consequences unfold. |

Build contracts from a shared structure covering the parties, the work, payment or revenue share, deadlines, and outcomes. Let the city's actual needs drive offers. A café with a missed delivery may offer a paid run, change suppliers, or raise prices. A storm can create jobs for fishing crews, divers, mechanics, security teams, and local reporters at the same time. The city keeps moving when players pass on an offer.

## How city activity creates opportunities

Characters and institutions have schedules, resources, obligations, and relationships. Venues book performers and pay staff. Restaurants order from fishers and growers. Security firms staff events. A local reporter or creator can cover what happens through their own work. Reputation is local to the people and organizations affected, and it can open doors in another line of work.

For example, a waterfront festival needs seafood suppliers, performers, security, and coverage. If weather closes the marina, organizers can move the show, suppliers can renegotiate, and the boatyard can commission inspections and repairs. Players encounter the same event through different jobs, with outcomes that carry into the next day. Over time, relationships, local news, and rumors can reveal deeper connections in the city through ordinary play.

## Situations already unfolding in the city

Each situation starts from existing relationships, debts, rivalries, or business pressures. Characters can make deals and change their plans before the player arrives. Players can encounter the same situation through different jobs, decline to get involved, or return later to see what changed.

| Situation | How players enter it | What the city remembers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Friends on different paths | Two people who grew up together now owe favors to rival circles. One is trying to keep a nightlife business afloat and the other wants to leave the underground economy. A performer, security worker, or mutual friend can get pulled into a choice about whom to stand by. | Who kept a promise, who earned a second chance, and who can still call on the neighborhood for help. |
| Rival crews across port towns | Two trading towns have their own prominent families, crews, and customs. When a trusted broker loses influence, a marina operator, charter captain, or old associate can be asked to mediate a dispute that crosses town lines. | Which relationships survived, which partners changed sides, and who still receives offers in each town. |
| A syndicate takes over a district | An underground organization gains leverage over tenants and struggling businesses. Owners, tenants, local reporters, and investigators each see a different part of the problem. Neighborhood groups can organize even if the player never joins them. | Which shops stayed open, who was displaced, what became public, and which institutions gained or lost trust. |
| An apprentice reads the room | A low-status apprentice notices contradictions that powerful people overlook. The player can help compare accounts, win someone’s trust, or help the apprentice secure a way out of a dangerous obligation. | Which characters trust the apprentice, which accounts proved credible, and who can move on to a different livelihood. |
| A discovery divides the coast | A fishing crew turns up a clue tied to a disputed piece of local history. Waterfront residents, resort developers, a family, and a museum each have competing claims. Fishing, diving, reporting, and archival work uncover different pieces of the dispute. | Who gets credited, what becomes public, where the find ends up, and how access to the coast changes. |
| A crew tests its loyalties | A disputed relic becomes the objective of a fictional crew caper. Play centers on recruiting characters with competing motives, choosing roles, improvising during scripted reversals, and deciding what to do with the relic and split afterward. | Who honored the agreement, which alliances survived, and who will work together on future jobs. |

One event can carry across several situations. A coastal discovery could prompt an interview commission, a salvage bid, a museum dispute, and a security job at the same time. Each is a playable line of work. The city’s reactions can also carry clues into the broader story, while leaving the major reveals for players to discover.

### A district under pressure

In an old market district, a syndicate's growing influence leaves local businesses and families facing hard choices about whom they can depend on. One shop closes early. A venue loses its regular suppliers. Tenants start organizing after several families leave the block. These changes appear in ordinary play before anyone offers a mission.

- **Residents and organizers** can help displaced households, rebuild neighborhood services, or bring residents and merchants together around a shared demand.
- **Shop owners** can join a merchants' association, accept a short-term deal that strains neighborhood trust, or keep a business open by changing suppliers and renegotiating contracts.
- **Reporters and creators** can follow the story through storefront closures and interviews. Publishing a corroborated account can bring public attention and new support. Getting the story wrong can cost sources their trust.
- **Investigators** can compare fictional case records and witness accounts. Strong cases and community trust can support an intervention, while a premature public accusation can cause witnesses to withdraw.
- **People trying to leave the syndicate** can take legitimate work through existing contacts. Their choices affect which former allies and neighborhood businesses will deal with them later.

Track which businesses survive, whether families return, and who residents trust. If the syndicate loses influence, recovery takes time. Vacant stores create opportunities for new owners, unresolved debts can divide former partners, and a new group can compete for influence. The district keeps changing even when the player is elsewhere.

### When local fame tests a friendship

Three friends built a following by hosting late-night shows at neighborhood workshops. When a waterfront venue offers a residency, the promoter gives one friend the authority to hire the crew and publicly credits them for the entire operation. That friend likes the attention. Another wants the original crew to share the opportunity, and the third is already negotiating a solo offer. The player can encounter that tension through a booking, a production job, or a local interview.

- A performer can negotiate shared billing, take a solo slot, or bring the crew to a smaller venue on terms they set together.
- A technician or organizer can broker a new agreement over pay, credit, and who gets to make decisions. Their relationships help determine who will hear them out.
- A reporter or creator can cover the scene as it grows. What they publish can prompt public support, harden a rivalry, or give a neglected collaborator a new opportunity.

Track public recognition, trust within the crew, and reliability with venues separately. A public claim can bring bigger offers while costing the player the people willing to help deliver the show. Broken promises can affect future bookings, while making amends takes follow-through. If the player walks away, the friends can still reconcile, split into rival acts, or find different work as the city moves on.

### A block that laughs at itself

The Pearl Steps street market hosts a live neighborhood broadcast where vendors, comics, and residents poke fun at local status games and city hall. A council candidate turns a sidewalk repair into a ribbon-cutting ceremony while the vendors quietly move their stalls around the hole. Players can book performers, film their own segment, sponsor the show, or play the straight-faced organizer trying to keep it on schedule.

A joke that lands can draw a crowd, boost sales, or give the organizer a recurring booking. A careless joke at a resident's expense can cost a sponsor or a longtime collaborator. Build the humor around local personalities, everyday contradictions, and powerful people taking themselves too seriously.

### What it costs to stay

In the same neighborhood, an apprentice boat mechanic is saving for a certification course. A longtime friend expects them to stand with the group after a public feud puts their reputation under pressure. Family, coworkers, and friends make competing claims on their time. The player can encounter the situation as a friend, employer, or mentor. They can offer paid work, help keep the apprenticeship open, or bring the people involved together before the dispute takes another turn.

Let the consequences show up in daily life. Someone stops turning up for shifts, a family changes its plans, a shop loses an apprentice, or a coworker offers a route back. Characters can act on their own, and later encounters reflect the promises they kept or broke. Give the market's comedy and the apprenticeship story room to breathe, especially when the neighborhood is dealing with a loss.
