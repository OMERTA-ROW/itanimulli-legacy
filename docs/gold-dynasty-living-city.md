# Gold Dynasty — Living City, Contracts, and Story

## Everyday play

Players can walk into shops, cafés, venues, workshops, and other accessible interiors and use them as part of daily life. Order food to restore health or drink coffee for a short energy boost and faster stamina recovery. Keep survival light so players can spend their time on the activities they choose. Doors, vehicles, equipment, and everyday objects should support meaningful interactions where the world allows them.

## Contracts and livelihoods

Let players earn money, make connections, switch careers, work multiple jobs, own businesses, hire other characters, and award contracts themselves. A one-off job can become recurring work, a partnership, or a bidding opportunity. Players can build a satisfying life in any of these paths.

| Role or industry | Example work and progression |
| --- | --- |
| Agriculture and food | Harvest and sell produce, supply a café, bid for hotel accounts, or build a food brand and hire growers. |
| Fishing and marine trades | Fish for leisure or income, sell a catch to a restaurant, fulfill a seafood supply deal, captain a charter, or run a boatyard. |
| Diving and salvage | Inspect a yacht, recover lost cargo, survey a wreck, or manage a dive crew. |
| Treasure hunting | Accept a museum commission, fund a private expedition, or negotiate with a family over a relic they claim. |
| Entertainment | Perform at a club, earn a residency, compose for a production, negotiate sponsorships or label deals, or hire and book performers. |
| Content creation and news | Film local stories, cover events, take commissions, build an audience, report on an unfolding incident, or run a production company. |
| Security | Take personal protection or event shifts, form a firm, staff jobs, and bid on work for festivals, estates, marinas, or ports. |
| Repair and transport | Restore classic cars, rent out vehicles or equipment, run deliveries, or expand into a garage or dispatch business. |
| Underground economy | Fictional syndicate contacts can offer waterfront or citywide courier jobs. Reputation, debts, promises, and relationships determine who offers work and how the consequences unfold. |

Build contracts from a shared structure covering the parties, the work, payment or revenue share, deadlines, and outcomes. Let the city's actual needs drive offers. A café with a missed delivery may offer a paid run, change suppliers, or raise prices. A storm can create jobs for fishing crews, divers, mechanics, security teams, and local reporters at the same time. The city keeps moving when players pass on an offer.

## The story beneath the city

Begin with a city already at work. Build an original overarching story beneath the ordinary activity. A fishing crew, a venue operator, a reporter, and a relic hunter can encounter different pieces of a shared mystery through the work they naturally choose. News, rumors, repeat clients, discoveries, and changing relationships can carry clues between careers. Let players discover the deeper story at their own pace and keep the central reveals out of everyday contract descriptions.

As new creative references come up, turn broad ideas such as power struggles, conflicting loyalties, and hidden histories into original Gold Dynasty roles, mechanics, and situations. Use the game's own characters, locations, dialogue, and plot.

## Maritime folklore and online events

The Lantern Census is a lost harbor ledger tying shipwreck gold to family claims and dock rights. The Sunken Court is the drowned chamber where those claims were once heard. A routine fishing charter or salvage contract can surface the first clue. Waterfront families, port operators, and salvagers pursue the claims on their own, and players can follow the dispute through jobs, rumors, and local news.

Turn up the supernatural for selected online events. During the Lantern Tide, harbor lights appear offshore in the footprints of a drowned district. Ships from different eras come through the fog and a staircase opens into the Sunken Court. Captains steer crews through changing channels, divers retrieve evidence, and reporters piece together whose claim will hold up when the tide turns. Crews can cooperate to recover the ledger or back rival claimants. The winning claim changes which faction offers salvage and dock contracts for the next few in-game days. Ordinary fishing and charters continue outside the event area.

## Playable childhood and coming of age

Use childhood chapters and side stories to turn broad movie premises into original Gold Dynasty play. Give young characters agency through observation, improvisation, friendships, and decisions about money. Carry the results into adulthood through family assets, neighborhood trust, and future contract offers.

- **The Boatyard After Dark.** During a waterfront festival, a kid notices someone using the commotion to move disputed boats out of the family's yard. Use the yard's floodlights, bells, and PA system to draw witnesses, involve neighbors, and get the harbor dispatcher to check the paperwork. The outcome shapes whether the family keeps the business and which neighbors they'll trust.
- **A Marina for the Summer.** A wealthy kid gets a summer budget and a vacant stretch of family-owned waterfront. Turn it into a go-kart track, a youth music venue, or a junior sailing club. Hire through an adult trustee, settle competing offers from friends and relatives, and see which local relationships survive once the summer money runs out.
- **The Disputed Payout.** A young character discovers a salvage payment credited to their family and an old contract suggesting the money is owed to several dockworkers. Bring the dispute to a trustee, negotiate a share for the families, or propose investing the settlement in a neighborhood business. The choice changes who becomes a mentor, rival, or future business partner.

## Coastal mysteries, strange jobs, and space

- **The Missing Mile.** Young friends from different sides of the harbor find conflicting shoreline surveys. One shows an inhabited strip of coast erased from modern charts. Following old ferry logs and family stories leads to a sealed tide station. The crew has to decide who gets the evidence when it could reopen public access or restore a family's claim to the waterfront.
- **The Night Shift.** A ferry cleaner finds wet footprints inside a locked cabin. A hotel laundry worker finds an obsolete room key sewn into a uniform. A dock clerk logs a cargo arrival with tomorrow's date. Most shifts are ordinary work. Selected contracts let players compare records across jobs and discover that the same offshore signal coincides with each incident. During online festivals, crews can investigate from different workplaces while the rest of the city carries on.
- **The Star Ledger.** At the tide station, a wall chart links old harbor lights to constellations. The stars tell players when to watch, and a repeating radio signal points to a stranded survey ship. Players who pursue the lead can take a rescue contract that opens the space layer. The crew charts a route, repairs life support, and decides who gets the recovered navigation data. Local folklore gives the constellation pattern its meaning, while navigation and rescue depend on astronomy and crew decisions.

### Passenger ships and orbital entertainment

**The Long Berth.** In the later space chapters, a passenger liner doubles as a touring venue and a lifeline for small stations that lose their regular ferry service. The same ship carries performers, maintenance crews, families, and temporary workers. Players can board on a paid contract as a stage technician, steward, journalist, or manifest clerk. Wages, tickets, and port fees use orbital credits. An onboard presenter runs a live call-in show from the observation deck, so a crew dispute or an unscheduled rescue can become the story that people back on the stations hear that night.

On a sold-out route, a frontier port learns that the liner is about to drop its only passenger stop. A shipboard inspector asks who authorized the change, and a stage crew discovers that some of the people in the audience depend on that berth to get home. Players can trace the competing decisions, bring people to the microphone, or find a way to fund another stop. The liner sails on its schedule and the affected settlements adjust even if the player stays ashore. In online play, a holiday broadcast can give crews in different ports simultaneous roles as performers, ship workers, local reporters, and relief crews. This gives the space layer its own entertainment scene, passenger routes, and local mysteries as those regions open across the trilogy.

### Orbital salvage and repair crews

**The Breaker Yards.** Once independent stations open, crews earn orbital credits recovering spent cargo pods, broken relays, and decommissioned modules. Take a shift as a tug pilot, yard appraiser, fabricator, or dispatcher. Pay out the crew's shares after berth and repair costs, and choose whether to sell what comes back or refurbish it for another contract. Yards supply parts to the passenger liner, station utilities, and small shops. Regular recoveries keep those services running and give a crew work between rescues and larger story events.

One recovery brings back a retired greenhouse module. A yard buyer has paid for its fittings, former growers claim the seed trays inside, and a frontier station needs the module to replace a crop bay lost to a storm. The crew can verify the claims, take a refit contract, help finance a replacement, or leave the dispute to the port court. Its decision affects produce prices, yard payroll, and who can afford passage on the next liner. Online crews can take parallel jobs recovering the module, preparing a berth, or setting up a temporary market while the station waits. Start with independent ports and let the same trade reach imperial charter disputes in Game 3.

## Mountain livelihoods and stories

Give the high country daily traffic. Guides lead hikes, lodge owners stock up for weekends, weather observers check ridge stations, and rescue crews cover the routes. Give each recurring character a livelihood, a personal goal, a relationship that matters, and a decision they'll make if the player never shows. Use film and television for broad premises such as a high-altitude rescue or a hiking group split over whether to turn back. Write the characters, terrain, conflicts, and outcomes for Gold Dynasty.

- **Last Light Pass.** A guide hired for an ordinary ridge walk hears a distress call as the weather closes in. The lodge owner wants guests back before dark, the rescue coordinator has to choose which route to cover, and a competing guide offers a shortcut. Players can join the response, relay trail conditions, or help hikers reach shelter. The rescue proceeds even if players pass on the job. Its outcome affects trail access and who gets guiding work.
- **The Old Switchback.** Hikers discover a route that used to connect two mountain towns. A lodge owner wants to reopen it, a family has been maintaining part of it, and a land buyer wants control of the trailhead. Different characters bring different information and act on their interests. A public crossing could bring visitors and supply contracts to both towns. Private control could send that business to a single lodge and force guides onto longer routes.
- **The Second Beacon.** During a rare online event, a light answers from a lookout that burned down decades ago. Storm clouds cut off helicopters, so hiking crews, weather observers, and lodge staff coordinate from different elevations. Old weather records connect the beacon to the Star Ledger, giving the mountains their own way into the orbital storyline.

## Mountain ecosystems, canyon folklore, and deep space

**Weather and wildlife.** Keep the rules easy to read. One storm can bring rain to the foothills, fog to the ridges, and whiteout conditions at the highest elevations. Weather changes visibility, footing, and which routes crews can use. Wildlife stays in its usual habitat and adjusts locally by sheltering, changing activity hours, or using nearby paths. A closed trail can reroute guiding or rescue work while animals remain in the same part of the mountain. Hiking, lodge supply runs, wildlife surveys, and weather-station work keep the region active between rare encounters.

- **Canyons — The Echo Steps.** Canyon towns tell stories about bridge trolls. Rescue crews start finding immense stone steps that local families attribute to the Nephilim. During a night search, a voice from an unmarked ledge repeats a message the crew only just heard over the radio. The crew can follow it, check it against old route records, or finish the search and turn the recording over to someone who knows the canyon stories.
- **Highest ice — The Ice Observatory.** At the coldest elevations, an ice survey exposes an ancient observatory that local records associate with the Anunnaki. Its instruments track the same sky pattern as the Star Ledger, but one reading is changing in real time. During a rare online whiteout, station staff can keep the instruments alive while climbing crews reach the exposed chamber and compare readings with the Second Beacon. Their combined observations reveal a narrow launch window and coordinates for a distant station.
- **Deep space — The Dark Transit.** A salvage crew docks at the distant station just before an eclipse. Two alien species are pursuing each other through the station. One navigates by vibration, and the other follows changes in heat. Restoring power lights the way to the dock, but also restarts the station's machinery and heating. Online crews can scout, manage power, and coordinate evacuation as they decide whether to finish the recovery job or leave with the people they've found. The escape or survival story can follow the crew to a remote world where another eclipse changes which routes are open and which settlements will take them in.

## Frontier prison and survival

**The Salt Line.** The Star Ledger opens a route into a remote system where mining settlements, freight crews, and a prison contractor already trade, argue over water rights, and move people between stations. Players can enter as a freight pilot, a field medic, or a detainee contesting a transfer. A dust surge grounds a prison transport in a salt basin. The first people who can reach it are local pump operators, and they have their own reason to bargain. The contractor has been diverting water from their settlement.

The basin heats quickly in daylight, then contracts after sunset, opening and closing different crossings. Local burrowers respond to coolant leaking from the grounded transport, so a crew may have to choose between keeping the medical bay cold and drawing wildlife away from the people waiting outside. Supply crews can track the weather, find shelter, or arrange a pickup. People stranded with the player have their own priorities. A guard wants to account for everyone aboard, a detainee wants the transfer manifest made public, and a pump operator wants a water agreement before the next convoy arrives. They can make deals and leave on their own timetable.

**Online play.** During rare basin-wide dust surges, separate crews can run supplies, relay landing conditions, document the contractor's actions, or evacuate people. Those decisions affect who controls the next convoy and which settlements get water deliveries. Crews arriving through the Star Ledger can take up this grounded survival story after The Dark Transit or find it through an ordinary freight contract.

## A ship and a crew of misfits

**The Long Way Home.** After The Salt Line, an independent freight captain offers passage aboard the Spare Hour, a converted market barge with a galley that doubles as a neighborhood radio studio. A former prison dispatcher brings the transfer records to clear the names of people listed as missing. A pump mechanic wants to keep the settlements supplied with water. The captain is trying to earn back docking rights at the port that seized the ship's old berth. Players can take shifts aboard, recruit crew through contracts, and earn a stake in the ship. Crew members accept or turn down jobs based on who gets paid and how the work affects people they know. If the player leaves, the ship keeps taking work and its roster can change.

**First job.** Bring a mining town's water-pump parts to a port built inside a spinning freight wheel. The vendor will only release the shipment at the next market bell, and a street band is broadcasting over the same channel the crew needs for docking. Players can work with the band to relay docking calls, present the disputed freight receipt to the market council, or broker a swap with another ship. The crew's choices affect who gets the pump parts and who opens their docks to the Spare Hour next time.

**Tone and online play.** Let humor come from personalities, clashing priorities, and how the crew reacts when a simple job gets messy. Use original in-world music from port stations and player performances. Online, friends can take different shipboard jobs, negotiate on the ground, or fly alongside in a second ship. Over time their contracts can connect remote settlements with the city through trade, music, and people returning home.

## The orbital empire and the succession

The full political storyline takes place in Game 3. The first two games can feature orbital jobs, rescues, and independent space settlements, with clues to the empire embedded across all regions.

**The Meridian Empire.** Build a living, Roman-inspired society across a capital station, provincial ports, and frontier settlements. A senate controls budgets and citizenship petitions, an emperor grants charters, and provincial governors compete for the next succession. Fleet commanders need dockworkers and freight pilots to keep supply routes open. Public forums, markets, neighborhood associations, and port courts give ordinary residents ways to contest the decisions made above them. Gold pays tariffs and funds supply contracts. The water dispute from The Salt Line reaches the capital because imperial charters determine who can divert water and who can appeal.

**The Unrecorded Heir.** The Spare Hour answers a distress call from a marooned survey family and helps them repair their habitat and find a route back. A survivor carries an implanted memory of a succession oath that contradicts the capital's official record. The memory might preserve a real witness account, an altered recollection, or someone else's experience. Players can compare it with ship logs, old testimony, and the Star Ledger. The family's immediate needs drive the rescue while governors, archivists, and harbor associations pursue the evidence on their own.

**Rise of the Kings in orbit.** The recovered oath concerns who can claim the throne and what that ruler owes the provinces under the founding charter. Players can back a house, broker an alliance between ports, bring their evidence to a public hearing, or build enough standing to seek a seat on the council and eventually press their own claim. The succession changes which ports honor trade contracts, where fleets can dock, how gold revenues are shared, and who gets water or rescue funding. Family, trade, and alliance decisions from the first two games shape who will deal with the player in Game 3. The outcome of the succession is part of the trilogy’s final legacy.

**Online play.** A disputed succession can unfold while ordinary work continues. One crew keeps the stranded habitat running, another escorts witnesses, and a third brings frontier residents' testimony to the capital through its radio stations. A contested vote or charter dispute gives the crews a shared outcome that changes local contracts and alliances.

## From the city launch program to the intergalactic federation

The original lunar program establishes the public institutions, civilian crews, and contractors that later missions rely on. Decades later, a public space directorate and a privately financed launch company form a joint program. The directorate brings public funding, mission authority, and oversight. The company brings shipyards, reusable launch services, and a founder who can turn a launch into prime-time news. Their agreement leaves the ownership of mission records, communications infrastructure, and future rescue obligations open to dispute. Both institutions are fictional.

**The founder.** Working name Imani Vey, a launch engineer who builds a private fleet around the public program. She wants to get stranded crews home and keep her ships flying. She also wants her company to control the routes it opens. Her first credible contact with nonhuman representatives comes through a rescue negotiation. The people she meets have institutions, rival interests, and obligations of their own. Vey can make promises on behalf of her company. Whether she can speak for Earth becomes a political fight. The restorer and the investigator reach her story from the civilian recovery records, and Vey has to decide what she will put on the record when those claims come due.

| Game | Vey and the main story | What society can see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Game 1 — The public program | Vey takes a public test-flight contract and asks the city for a new launch terminal. The waterfront hearing brings the old contractor's records back into view. The restorer and the player can establish whose work is missing from the official account. | A launch on the evening news, jobs at the terminal, a council vote about public funding, and rumors about a rescue call nobody will claim. |
| Game 2 — The recovery corridor | A relay job and independent port records establish that there is a working off-world route with rules and counterparties. Vey expands from launch contractor to corridor operator under a provisional agreement. The investigator follows the payroll case while workers and port officials press their own claims. | Independent settlements, salvage wages in orbital credits, rescue obligations, and limited evidence of contact. The public arguments on Earth concern who authorized the agreement and who is paying for it. |
| Game 3 — The federation | The charter gala heist and succession hearing establish which agreements survived the change in power. Delegations from nonhuman polities arrive with their own positions. Vey's dealings come into the open, and Earth has to decide who represents it. | The intergalactic federation becomes publicly visible and playable through its assembly, treaty ports, and shared dispute forums. Meridian is a power within it, with its own succession and provincial duties. Other members can bargain or disagree with Meridian and Earth. |

Keep the federation's daily life visible through docking rights, trade in orbital credits, rescue dispatch, route permits, translation work, and hearings over obligations between member societies. Earth still has its own elections, courts, local wages, businesses, and family disputes. A federation vote can change which routes a port serves while a council back home is still arguing about whether to replace the slipway.

### What the city builds and what the next generation inherits

Carry change forward through named sites and households. An old freight shed can become a terminal parts depot under one administration or reopen as a public workshop under another. A building going up creates survey, trade, and supply contracts. When it opens, businesses relocate, rents and routes change, and older workers can recognize what stood there before. Let construction progress on the calendar through budgets, hiring, delays, and hearings. Keep enough of the old street plan and businesses visible for returning players to recognize the place.

Founding families have separate businesses, debts, relationships, and public offices. A family fight over a contractor's accounts can become a public story when workers go unpaid or a relative speaks at a hearing. Corruption, suspected laundering, and underground drug trade have visible consequences through contested books, seized assets, strained households, clinics, courts, and elections. Show how money and political favors affect lawful jobs and public services. Let workers, reporters, investigators, and relatives pursue their own interests, and resolve local cases on their own evidence. A construction audit can corroborate the launch contractor's accounts when relevant. Other scandals and family fights can play out through news or ordinary work.

At each game's close, carry forward the contracts people kept, who controls important sites, who holds office, which debts or testimony are still disputed, and what a household or player-built enterprise can pass on. Show old decisions through later jobs, access, alliances, and places people can visit. For example, a vote that preserves a public workshop in Game 1 can leave Game 2 with a trained repair crew and Game 3 with a locally owned supplier that can compete for a port contract. NPCs make their own decisions when players miss a vote or a case. Design the three-game arc to resolve its heists, recovery claims, and succession. Later installments, if pursued, can inherit those legacies and follow the next generation of disputes.

## The trilogy's slow reveal

- **Game 1 — Rumors across the map.** City vendors hear conflicting stories about who owns an old port charter. A canyon guide recognizes a mark that also appears on a salvage receipt. A freight crew uses a call sign nobody at the dock recognizes. Each clue has a local context, and none confirms the Meridian Empire. Ordinary jobs and regional stories provide their own payoffs.
- **Game 2 — Evidence that almost fits.** A repeater high in the mountains echoes a transmission logged during a coastal job. A redacted freight manifest and competing witness accounts suggest that someone coordinated events across regions. Players can investigate and reach different conclusions. The empire's capital, senate, and succession remain out of sight.
- **Game 3 — The empire comes into view.** The Unrecorded Heir brings the rescue, implanted memory, and competing records into the same case. The Meridian Empire's capital and institutions become accessible, and players can verify which earlier clues were related. Reporters, witnesses, and returning crew members give newcomers enough context to follow the story, while returning players can recognize clues and use relationships they built earlier.

The city, coast, mountains, and frontier each have standalone livelihoods, conflicts, and endings. Scatter optional clues through routine contracts, news, local lore, and online events. Early online encounters can deepen a rumor or preserve a piece of evidence; the public reveal and playable succession begin in Game 3.

### The first launch and its unfinished record

Open with a short archival countdown from Gold Dynasty's first crewed lunar landing. The public broadcast ends on the landing announcement. Then jump to the present-day city, where the same recording plays during an anniversary event as shops open and crews report to work. The historic landing anchors the setting. A fictional follow-up mission, flown decades later, gives the trilogy its disputed record. Its official account says a remote relay was written off. A recovery crew's payroll ledger shows Earth wages for work that continued after the closure date and a separate line for an unfamiliar off-world settlement unit.

- **Game 1 — The anniversary.** Take a broadcast, museum, or archive job, or follow a tip from a retired launch worker's family. Players can recover missing credit or pay for members of the ground crew. The ledger entry can stand on its own as a bookkeeping oddity. The waterfront and other city stories still carry the present-day opening.
- **Game 2 — Field reports near the relay.** A survey crew reports moving figures visible only in one instrument feed near a retired relay site. The optional investigation can start with a job to find an overdue crew and help them home. Weather, equipment logs, and witness accounts support different readings. An old maintenance recording gives curious players another way to follow the relay's history. The nearby private bunker is part of the region's ordinary contract work and local gossip.
- **Game 3 — Who paid for the route.** Orbital salvage contracts bring players to the kind of independent yard that kept early relays serviceable. Surviving manifests and port-bank records show how crews once settled recovery and resupply bills between ports, and how those arrangements grew into the internal credit network players now use. Compare that history with the charter and reserve archives when the capital opens. Keep the cause of the field sightings and the truth of the gold-exchange rumor open until their own evidence supports a conclusion.

The bunker can have its own ending through its residents, staff, and host town. The first launch gives all three games a shared piece of history, while most people encounter it in the course of ordinary work.

### A false alarm at the private bunker

In an optional Game 2 story, someone convinces a billionaire consortium that an orbital breakup is imminent. The members rush to their private bunker and a local presenter repeats the claim on air. The predicted breakup never happens. Most players can experience the whole incident as a news clip, a late supply shift, or a joke people tell after the guests come back out.

Players who follow up can compare the broadcast with the observatory's public record or hear from a bunker employee who was called in that night. The local fallout can include unpaid overtime, a correction on the evening news, and guests asking who benefited from getting them underground. The bunker keeps its regular maintenance and supply work, and the incident passes on the world calendar without the player. Leave the instigator's motive open for later writing.

## Neighborhood mysteries and everyday rivalries

- **The Night Desk — supernatural detective cases.** Two junior investigators run an after-hours missing-person desk in a shuttered train station. At midnight, a ghost can replay one thing they heard in life, from their own imperfect point of view. A player can interview families, check old timetables, or work a night shift to get a second account. An opening case follows a message left on a platform demolished years ago and ends with a family deciding what to do with the truth.
- **Vesper Works — corporate mystery.** A trusted contractor runs clinics, emergency shelters, and weather stations. Drivers deliver supplies, technicians service equipment, and reporters cover public contracts. After a storm, a family finds that the company listed its occupied block as empty. Players can compare relocation records, hear out workers and residents, and decide where the evidence goes. The outcome can reopen a clinic or change who runs the district's emergency contracts. Later regional cases can show competing branches and employees with different loyalties.
- **The Midnight Auction — caper.** A touring band, a stagehand, and a venue operator want to establish which family owns a gold instrument going up for auction. Work the event as a performer, photographer, or catalog assistant. Each role hears a different piece of the ownership story. The ruling changes which venue gets the instrument and where the band plays next.
- **The Floodlight Cup — neighborhood rivalry.** During storm season, two districts enter a night go-kart series with their repair shops and local sponsors on the line. Race, tune a kart, commentate, or book the next event as a promoter. The winner earns the right to host the next round, bringing customers and temporary jobs to that district.

Each arc has a local payoff and recurring work. Some cases end as family disputes, neighborhood rivalries, or company decisions. Others can leave an optional clue for the later trilogy mystery. In online sessions, friends can contribute from distinct jobs and share the outcome without taking the same role.

## The Return Route — a story across the map

Open on a beach weekend with a surf competition, boardwalk shows, food stands, and charter boats. Work as a lifeguard, race the surf course, play a set, or take a water-taxi shift. A passing storm washes an old route marker onto the beach with fresh names painted over its numbers. The festival and its jobs carry on while a harbor reporter and a missing courier's family begin asking questions.

Use backroad horror for isolation, a detour that feels wrong, and evidence with several plausible readings. Some incidents have already happened. Players investigate the remnants, decide whose account to trust, and choose what gets reported or which road reopens. Resolve the courier case on its own terms. Treat the Night Desk and Vesper Works' strangest material as rare, discoverable side stories. Some leads can touch the wider mystery, but the hidden cases can have their own explanations and local consequences.

## Regional banks, local branches, and vaults

Give every region one or two flagship banks with large, explorable vaults. Build a denser network of smaller banks, credit unions, and branch offices in neighborhoods, market towns, marinas, and settlements. Downtown and other busy hubs can support two major institutions and several neighborhood banks. Smaller communities get local counters or cooperative branches near the work people already do.

| Region | Flagship vaults | Smaller banks and branches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City and industrial waterfront | Two banks hold gold reserves, business collateral, and family deposits. | Market branches handle payroll and merchant accounts. Neighborhood credit unions finance repairs and storefronts. |
| Beach and boardwalk | A coastal bank holds resort escrow, family valuables, and seasonal business reserves. | Boardwalk and neighborhood branches serve performers, food stands, and seasonal workers. |
| Harbor and islands | A maritime bank holds trade gold, charter deposits, salvage bonds, and shipping records. | Marina branches and island cooperatives serve fishing crews, boatyards, and ferry operators. |

Players can deposit earnings and gold, rent a safe-deposit box, apply for business financing, or take jobs in accounting, appraisal, and vault inventory. Flagship vaults get multi-room interiors with distinctive architecture and story visits. Local branches spend their time on ordinary business, and closures or funding decisions can affect which shops and crews get paid. A disputed lockbox or deposit ledger can start an investigation with its own local outcome.

## Orbital credits, income, and federal authority

Earth uses local money for wages, rent, shops, and regional contracts. Orbital settlements use their own internal currency, provisionally called orbital credits, for docking, provisions, housing, repairs, and crew payroll. Explore making each orbital credit worth more per unit than Earth money, with the exchange rate and local prices left for playtesting. A stronger unit still buys only what local wages and prices allow. At ports that serve both economies, banks quote the exchange before a player confirms it. Gold can be appraised and sold or held as collateral. Balances stay in their original currency until exchanged.

Players can build an income in orbit through freight, salvage, repair, mining, rescue, or ordinary station work. Frontier banks and station credit unions handle crew pay, savings, and loans in orbital credits. A player can arrive with Earth savings, earn credits on a first contract, or combine both to cover a berth and the next trip. ### Game 1 follow-on — The winter bank case

During the first winter market after the hearing, a robbery makes the evening news in a small inland town with a large regional bank. The bank financed the music hall's original renovation and holds the town's winter payroll accounts. The robbery can happen offscreen. Someone in custody has spent months telling different versions of a story about that bank. When the robbery makes the news, they recognize a detail they shared in one version. A person who heard that version was recently released. The tipster has a reason to reach out and a reason to leave part of the story out. A family member might remember a line from an ordinary call. ## Game 3 finale — The charter gala heist

At a televised gala in the Meridian capital, a donor household plans to unveil a ceremonial edition of the founding charter. The original register it borrowed for the exhibition carries witness attestations and obligations owed to frontier ports. The returning restorer and a provincial archivist propose a heist to reclaim the register before the household's loan expires and the exhibit leaves the capital. Its public reading could give affected ports standing in the succession hearing. Some witnesses' families want the terms read on air. Others want their accounts entered with privacy protections. Give the restorer a personal temptation to take credit publicly after years of being known by rumor.

Build the set piece around the reveal, with roles in the gala crew, the broadcast booth, or the archivist's team. Players can help recover the register and choose to bring it to a public reading, entrust it to a coalition of port witnesses, or enter it under seal. The original hearing still weighs testimony and decides how much authority the register carries alongside the disputed oath. Let the outcome affect who controls the register and who is credited, and let decisions at the hearing affect port agreements and services. A newcomer can learn the stakes from the featured ports and the gala. Returning players see how the restorer handles a second public promise after the music hall case. Preserve the exact gala reversal and the instigator's private decisions for later writing.
